Image sources for the lecture on the training loop
===================================================

Every figure in this folder is an original plot in the course palette (`notebooks/plotting.py`),
generated by the scripts in `../figures/`, one or more per part of the lecture; `../make_figures.py`
runs them all. The figures have a transparent background, so the colour of the slide shows
through. The diagrams of the slides are TikZ pictures written inline in the `.tex` file. The
scripts print every number that the slides quote.

Part 1: data and representation
-------------------------------

Scripts: `../figures/part1_objects.py` (the six objects of the first slide; a few seconds),
`../figures/part1_representations.py` (tokens and positions, two rings, Fourier features;
about ten minutes), `../figures/part1_normalization.py` (a skewed cloud, standardized; a
second) and `../figures/part1_mnist.py` (the digits; about three minutes). The
scripts print every number that the slides of part 1 quote.

- `representation_examples.pdf`: six objects side by side and a map `phi` into some `R^d` for
  each, drawn at the size it has on the slide (120 mm, a little wider than the text). Every
  array at the bottom is computed from the object above it: two matrices, three vectors
  drawn with cells of one height, and a spectrogram as tall as the vectors. From left to right:
  - The digit is a smooth curve through nine points, placed in the central 12 x 12 pixels of a
    16 x 16 image; each pixel is as dark as the pen covers it (fully within 0.45 pixels of the
    curve, not at all beyond 1.25).
  - The molecule is 4-aminophenol, `HO-C6H4-NH2`, with its hydrogens left out: eight atoms
    (grey carbon, orange oxygen, blue nitrogen, the dots beside the rows of the matrix) and
    eight bonds, double bonds drawn but counted once in the adjacency matrix.
  - The pendulum is at `theta = 35` degrees with `theta' = -1.2`; its state is written as
    `(cos theta, sin theta, theta') = (0.82, 0.57, -1.2)` in `R^3`, which keeps the angle on
    the circle.
  - The word "cat" is token 9246 of the 50,257 of GPT-2 (the `encoder.json` of the item
    "Tokens and positions" below); its one-hot vector `e_9246` is drawn short, as
    `0, 0, ..., 1, 0, 0`.
  - The function is `f(x) = sin(pi x + 0.5)`, one oscillation on `[-1, 1]` (the phase keeps
    both odd and even coefficients; the target `g` of lecture 02 is odd, so half of its
    coefficients vanish). The cells are its coefficients `<f, e_n>` in the orthonormal
    Legendre basis, computed with 64-point Gauss-Legendre quadrature, blue negative and orange
    positive. `<f, e_0>`, the mean of `f`, is 0, so the vector starts at `<f, e_1>`; five are
    drawn: 0.684, -0.461, -0.543, 0.132, 0.082. The next are -0.012, -0.005 and below 0.002
    from `n = 8` on.
  - The sound is a synthetic utterance, 0.9 s at 16 kHz, built as speech is: harmonics of a
    pitch that falls from 132 to 112 Hz and from 150 to 98 Hz in the two voiced stretches,
    with a slow random wander, shaped by four formants that glide between vowels ("ai", then
    "uoa"; Peterson and Barney's averages for F1-F3), a hiss between 3.8 and 7.6 kHz as in "s",
    a little breath noise falling with frequency and a faint white background (NumPy seed 0).
    Its representation is the input of Whisper (Radford et al., 2023): the log-mel
    spectrogram, 25 ms periodic Hann windows every 10 ms centred on the frame (reflection at
    the ends), the power spectrum pooled by 80 mel filters from 0 to 8 kHz (Slaney's scale and
    area normalization, the defaults of `librosa.filters.mel`), `log10`, floored 8 decades
    under its maximum, then `(x + 4) / 4`: 80 bands x 90 frames, from -0.53 to 1.47, shown
    from its maximum down to the floor. The script computes it with NumPy; recomputed from
    the same samples with librosa 1.0.1 (`librosa.stft` and `librosa.filters.mel`, as in
    `whisper/audio.py`) it agrees to `1e-8`. The waveform above and the spectrogram share
    their time axis.

  It replaces a drawing (page 3 of `images/plots.drawio` in the deck
  `2026-04_ml-for-mathematicians` of the presentations repository) that was exported as
  `representation_examples.png`.
- Tokens and positions (no figure; the slide "Text for a language model" draws them in TikZ).
  The sentence "The dog bites the man." is encoded with the byte-pair encoding of GPT-2: six
  tokens, one per word and one for the full stop, with indices 464, 3290, 26081, 262, 582 and
  13 in a vocabulary of 50,257. Every token but the first carries the space before its word
  (` dog`, ` bites`, ...); the slide does not draw that space. "The man bites the dog." gives
  the same six tokens in another order. The two files that define the tokenizer,
  `encoder.json` and `vocab.bpe`, are downloaded once to `notebooks/data/` from
  `https://openaipublic.blob.core.windows.net/gpt-2/models/124M/` and are not part of the
  repository. The script implements the merges itself; its word-splitting pattern agrees
  with the one of GPT-2 on plain ASCII text, and it reproduces the reference encoding of
  "Hello world", 15496 and 995. The embedding vectors `e_{i_t}` are learned; the slide draws
  them as random numbers in `[-1, 1]` (the random generator of TikZ, seed 2026), on the colour
  scale of `p_t`. The vectors `p_t` below them are the
  sinusoidal positions of Vaswani et al. (2017, section 3.5), `sin(w_j t)` and `cos(w_j t)` in
  neighbouring coordinates with `w_j = 10000^(-2j/d)`, drawn for `d = 8` and `t = 0, ..., 5`
  (blue -1, white 0, orange +1). TikZ computes them from the formula; the script prints the
  same values. The sizes quoted on that slide are those of GPT-3, which reuses the tokenizer
  of GPT-2: a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens and `d_model = 12288` (Brown et al., 2020,
  table 2.1).
- `rings_coordinates.pdf`: two rings of 200 points each, with radii `1 + 0.12 xi` and
  `2.2 + 0.12 xi` and uniform angles (NumPy seed 0). A linear score `a . z + b` is fitted
  with the binary cross-entropy and Adam (learning rate 0.05, 2000 full-batch steps,
  PyTorch seed 0) on `z = (x_1, x_2)` and on `z = (r, theta)`; the black line is its zero
  set. It classifies 53.5% of the points correctly in Cartesian coordinates and 100% in
  polar ones.
- `normalization.pdf`: 1000 points in `R^2` with independent skew-normal coordinates
  (Azzalini's construction, NumPy seed 0): `x_1` with location 20, scale 30 and shape 8
  (mean 43.1, standard deviation 18.1, skewness 1.07), `x_2` with location 5, scale 3 and
  shape 4 (mean 7.4, standard deviation 1.87, skewness 0.65). Left, the raw points; right,
  each coordinate standardized, `z_j = (x_j - mean_j) / sd_j`, with the sample mean and
  standard deviation. Both panels have equal axes; the cross is the mean and the dashed box
  the mean plus or minus one standard deviation per coordinate. The skewness is unchanged.
- `fourier_features.pdf`: a struck string, `u_tt = u_xx` on `[0, 1]` with
  `u(0, t) = u(1, t) = 0`, `u(x, 0) = exp(-((x - 0.3) / 0.04)^2)` and `u_t(x, 0) = 0`. Left,
  d'Alembert's solution `(F(x - t) + F(x + t)) / 2`, where `F` is the odd, 2-periodic
  extension of the bump, on a grid of 201 x 401 points of `[0, 1] x [0, 2]` (one period;
  blue -1, white 0, orange +1). A tanh network `. -> 64 -> 64 -> 1` (PyTorch seed 0) is fitted
  to 4000 samples of `u` at uniform random points of that box (NumPy seed 0) with Adam,
  learning rate `1e-3`, full batch. Its input is `(x, t)` (4,417 parameters) or the `4m`
  numbers `cos(j pi x), sin(j pi x), cos(j pi t / 2), sin(j pi t / 2)`, `j = 1, ..., m`
  (8,385 parameters for `m = 16`); the periods are twice the sides of the box, so the map is
  injective on it. Centre and right, the two fits after 3000 steps; the error in the titles
  is `||f - u|| / ||u||` on the grid, 83.8% and 13.9%. For the seeds 0 to 4: 83.8, 81.6,
  75.0, 72.6 and 69.6%, against 13.9, 13.8, 13.4, 14.8 and 15.2%. Checked every 500 steps,
  the plain input falls below 13.9% from step 12,000. After 20,000 steps it is at 2.9%
  (training error `1.9e-5`) and `m = 16` at 8.4% (training error `6.3e-6`): the harmonics
  fit faster, not better between the samples. After 3000 steps, `m = 1` gives 30.2%, `m = 4`
  19.3%, and `m = 64` 69.6% with a training error of `9.8e-6`.
- `mnist_pca.pdf`: principal component analysis of the 60,000 training images (the
  eigenvectors of their covariance matrix, each with its largest entry made positive). Left,
  the first two test images reconstructed from their first 8, 32, 128 and 784 components.
  Right, the test accuracy of the baseline network `k -> 16 -> 16 -> 10` trained on the first
  `k` components (same seeds, cross-entropy, Adam with learning rate `1e-3`, batches of 64,
  5 epochs), and the fraction of the variance that they keep. One run for each `k`: 47.6,
  63.9, 86.2, 91.5, 93.6, 94.1, 94.4 and 94.9% for `k = 2, 4, ..., 256`; the dotted line is
  the 94.0% of the 784 pixels. With `k = 64` the network has 1,482 parameters and the
  components keep 86.2% of the variance.
- `mnist_directions.pdf`: left, the first 16 principal directions of the training images;
  right, the 16 rows of the first weight matrix of the trained baseline `784 -> 16 -> 16 -> 10`
  of lecture 02. Each is a 28 x 28 image, divided by its largest absolute entry; red is
  positive and blue negative.
- Numbers without a figure, all from `part1_mnist.py` and all with the baseline network,
  loss and optimizer. (a) The inputs as bytes in `[0, 255]`, as grey levels in `[0, 1]` and
  with the mean training image subtracted, each for the initialization seeds 0, 1, 2 with
  shuffling seeds 1, 2, 3: mean test accuracy 89.5, 91.2 and 93.0% after one epoch, and
  92.7, 94.3 and 95.2% after five; seed 0 in `[0, 1]` is the run of lecture 02, 94.00%.
  (b) The largest eigenvalue of `(1/N) sum x x^T` is 38.09 for the pixels in `[0, 1]` and

- The table of the slide "How large is what the model sees?" quotes the papers: patches of

### Checks behind the frame "What depth buys: the parity of n bits" (`part2_depth.py`)

No figure, only assertions and printed counts:

- `T` composed `k` times is the zigzag through the points `(j / 2^k, j mod 2)`, hence `2^k`
  linear pieces, for `k = 1, ..., 10`.
- For `n = 2, 4, 8, 16` and all `2^n` inputs: parity equals `T^k(mean of the bits)` with
  `n = 2^k`; it equals the product of the signs `(-1)^bit`; a tournament bracket of 2-input XOR
  gates computes it with `n - 1` gates in `log2 n` levels (as neurons,
  `XOR(a, b) = relu(a - b) + relu(b - a)`, so `2(n - 1)` ReLU neurons); one hidden layer of `n`
  threshold neurons `1[s >= j]` added with signs `+, -, +, ...` computes it; and so does the
  network with `k` hidden layers of two ReLU neurons (first layer `relu(s / 2^k)` and
  `relu(s / 2^k - 1/2)`, then weights `2, -4` and biases `0, -1/2`). For `n <= 8` the OR of
  ANDs with one term per odd input is built as well: `2^(n-1)` terms, each accepting one input.
- For `n = 10` (all 1024 inputs) and `n = 20` (one input for each number of ones): `2^(n-1)`
  = 512 and 524,288 AND terms; 9 and 19 XOR gates; 10 and 20 threshold neurons; 8 ReLU neurons
  in 4 layers and 10 in 5 layers, with `k = ceil(log2 n)` and the input `s / 2^k`.

Part 3: loss
------------

The three figures of part 3 are original plots generated by `../figures/part3_loss.py` in the
course palette (`notebooks/plotting.py`). No external images are used. The script prints every
number that the slides of part 3 quote; it takes about a minute and a half on one CPU thread.

- `inverse_problem_losses.pdf`: the inverse of the target `g(x) = sin(3x) + 0.3x` of lecture
  02. Labels `y` are uniform on `[-1, 1]` and inputs are `x = g(y) + 0.05 xi` with standard
  Gaussian `xi`: 10,000 training samples (NumPy seed 0) and 20,000 test samples (seed 1).
  Since `g(1) = 0.441` and `max g = 1.162`, two values of `y` are compatible with `x` when
  `0.44 < |x| < 1.16`. Two networks with the same hidden layers, `1 -> 64 -> 64` with tanh
  (PyTorch seed 0), are trained with Adam, learning rate `1e-3`, batches of 64, 30 epochs
  (4,710 steps, shuffling seed 1): one with a single output and the squared loss (4,353
  parameters), one with 50 outputs, the softmax and the cross-entropy, the classes being the
  50 bins of width 0.04 of `[-1, 1]` (7,538 parameters). The exact conditional law is computed
  by Bayes' formula on a grid of 4,001 values of `y`.
  Left, a fifth of the training data, the squared-loss network and the exact conditional mean;
  the root mean squared error on the test set is 0.237 for both. Middle, the density of `y`
  given `x` predicted by the second network (softmax probabilities divided by the bin width).
  Right, that density at `x = 0.8` against the exact one, and the prediction 0.568 of the
  squared-loss network; the solutions of `g(y) = 0.8` are 0.268 and 0.856, and
  `g(0.568) = 1.162`. The mean log-density of the test labels is 1.51 for the histogram, 1.64
  for the exact law and 0.02 for a Gaussian centred at the squared-loss prediction whose
  constant variance is the training mean squared error, 0.0567. The predicted law puts a mass
  0.45 below the squared-loss prediction (exact: 0.48). The setting follows the inverse
  problems of Bishop (1994), with a different function.
- The digit classifier with another loss (numbers only, no figure): the `784 -> 16 -> 16 -> 10`
  network of lecture 02 trained by `common.train_mnist` (Adam, learning rate `1e-3`, batches
  of 64, 5 epochs, same seeds) with the cross-entropy, 94.00% test accuracy, and with the
  squared error on the softmax probabilities `|softmax(f) - e_y|^2`, 93.56%. With the
  cross-entropy, 600 test images are misclassified, 86 of them with a largest probability
  above 0.9.
- `rare_event_data.pdf`, `rare_event_metrics.pdf`: a synthetic rare event. A point is an
  anomaly with probability 0.01; normal points are `N(0, I)` in the plane and anomalies
  `N(mu, I)` with `mu = 3 (1, 1) / sqrt(2)`. 20,000 training points (NumPy seed 0, 197
  anomalies), 20,000 validation points (seed 1, 185 anomalies) and 1,000,000 test points
  (seed 2, 9,874 anomalies). The network `2 -> 16 -> 16 -> 1` (ReLU, PyTorch seed 0) is trained
  with the binary cross-entropy and Adam, learning rate `1e-3`, batches of 64, 20 epochs
  (shuffling seed 1); a second copy is trained with the anomalies weighted by `c = 99`
  (`pos_weight` of `BCEWithLogitsLoss`).
  `rare_event_data.pdf` shows the first 5,000 training points (51 anomalies) and the level set
  `p(x; w) = 1/2` of the first network. `rare_event_metrics.pdf` shows, on the test set,
  accuracy, precision, recall and F1 of the first network against the threshold `t` (300
  values between 0.001 and 0.999), and its precision-recall curve. The markers are `t = 1/2`;
  the threshold 0.415 that maximizes F1 on the validation set; `t = 1/(1 + c) = 0.01`; and the
  weighted network at `t = 1/2`. The last two classifiers agree on 98.89% of the test points,
  and the scores of the two networks differ by 4.67 in the median where `0.05 < q < 0.95`
  (`log 99 = 4.60`). The script also prints the exact values for the true posterior, from the
  Gaussian distribution function: the classifier of highest accuracy reaches 99.37% with
  recall 0.487.

Part 4: optimization
--------------------

Figures written by `../figures/part4_sgd.py` (about two minutes) and
`../figures/part4_mnist_batch.py` (about three minutes), in the course palette. The two
diagrams of this part (the four steps of the lecture and the training loop with its
controller) are TikZ pictures in the `.tex` file. The scripts print every number that the
slides quote.

The small problem of `sgd_stationary.pdf` and `noise_floor.pdf` is the least-squares fit of
8 hat functions to the labels `g(x_i) + 0.3 xi_i` at 160 equispaced points of `[-1, 1]`
(NumPy seed 0), with `F(w) = mean_i (phi_i . w - y_i)^2`. Its Hessian `H = 2 Phi^T Phi / N` has
`L = 0.276` and `mu = 0.075`; `F* = 0.078`. The covariance of the gradient of one sample at
the minimizer is `Sigma = (4 / N) sum_i r_i^2 phi_i phi_i^T` with the residuals `r_i` at
`w*`, and `tr Sigma = 0.2125`.

- `sgd_stationary.pdf`: SGD with a constant step from `w*`, indices drawn with replacement,
  400 independent runs (NumPy seed 0) for each of the 16 pairs `eta = 0.8, 0.2, 0.05, 0.0125`
  and `B = 1, 4, 16, 64`. With the time `t = k eta`, the first 40 units of time are discarded
  and the next 100 are used. Left, the histogram of the fourth coefficient minus its value at
  `w*`, sampled once per unit of time, for `(eta, B) = (0.0125, 1), (0.05, 4), (0.2, 16)`
  (ratio 0.0125) and `(0.05, 1)` (ratio 0.05); the dashed curves are the Gaussian densities
  of variance `S_44`, where `S` solves the Lyapunov equation `H S + S H = (eta / B) Sigma`.
  Right, the mean of `F(w_k) - F*` over the runs and the 100 units of time, against `eta / B`;
  the dashed line is `eta tr(Sigma) / 4B`. The measured value divided by the line is between
  0.998 and 1.042 for `eta <= 0.05`, between 1.023 and 1.151 for `eta = 0.2`, and 1.091,
  1.123, 1.243 and 2.306 for `eta = 0.8` with `B = 64, 16, 4, 1`. SGD with the constant
  covariance `Sigma` (the discrete Lyapunov equation
  `S = (I - eta H) S (I - eta H) + eta^2 Sigma / B`) gives 1.089 for `eta = 0.8`: this part of
  the deviation comes from the size of the step, the rest from the dependence of the noise on
  `w`.
- `noise_floor.pdf` (from lecture 02, same code and seeds): left, SGD from `w_0 = 0` on the
  same problem; each curve is the mean of 20 runs of 20,000 steps, with indices drawn with
  replacement. The mean gaps over the last 5000 steps are `4.1e-2`, `2.8e-3` and `1.8e-3`

### `../figures/part5_surprises.py` (about four minutes, nearly all of it the exact Hessians of the edge-of-stability figure)

- `overfitting_classical.pdf`: the double-descent experiment below, restricted to
  `m = 1, ..., 16` features, that is to the classical regime `m < N = 20`. The blue curve is
  the risk itself: the median over the 300 draws of the mean squared distance to the target on
  a grid of 2001 points, plus the noise level `s^2 = 0.04` (the expected squared error on a
  fresh noisy label); the band is its interquartile range. The grey curve is the mean training
  error. The dotted line is `s^2` and the vertical rule marks the best `m`. The script also
  checks the identity of the opening frame on the same draws: with `H` the projection onto the
  features at the samples and `b_m^2 = |(I - H) g|^2 / N`, the mean training error is
  `b_m^2 + s^2 (1 - m/N)` and the mean error on fresh labels at the same inputs is
  `b_m^2 + s^2 (1 + m/N)` (for `m = 4`: 0.0360 and 0.0521 measured, 0.0361 and 0.0521 from the
  formulas).
- `double_descent.pdf` (from lecture 02, code and seeds unchanged): Legendre features
  `P_0, ..., P_{m-1}`, 20 equispaced samples of the target with Gaussian noise of standard
  deviation 0.2. `numpy.linalg.lstsq` returns the least-squares fit for `m <= 20` and the
  interpolant of minimal coefficient norm for `m > 20`. Left, the median and the interquartile
  band of the test error over 300 draws of the noise (NumPy seed 0) and the mean training
  error; here the test error is the mean squared distance to the target on the grid, without
  the noise level. Right, three fits of one draw (seed 3).
- `edge_of_stability.pdf` (from lecture 02, unchanged): full-batch gradient descent on the
  mean squared error of a `1 -> 24 -> 24 -> 1` tanh network (673 parameters, double precision,
  PyTorch seed 0) on 40 equispaced samples of the target, 12,000 steps for
  `eta = 0.2, 0.1, 0.05`. The sharpness is the largest eigenvalue of the exact Hessian of the
  training loss, computed every 50 steps. The experiment follows Cohen, Kaur, Li, Kolter and
  Talwalkar (2021).
- `spectral_bias.pdf` (from lecture 02, unchanged): a `1 -> 64 -> 64 -> 1` tanh network
  (PyTorch seed 0) fitted to `g(x) + 0.3 sin(20x)` on 160 equispaced points with Adam, learning
  rate `1e-3`, full batch, 10,000 steps. The right panel is the projection of the residual on
  `sin(3x)` and on `0.3 sin(20x)`, each divided by the squared norm of that component.

### `../figures/part5_scale.py` (about five minutes: 24 training runs of 4,690 steps)

- `parameters_vs_data.pdf`: number of parameters `P` against the size `N` of the training set
  for eight models, with the line `P = N`. It replaces `model_scale.pdf` of lecture 02
  (parameters against year). `N` counts labelled examples: images for the classifiers; words or
  tokens for the language models, where every token is a prediction target. Sources:
  - LeNet-5: 60,000 trainable parameters, 60,000 MNIST training images (LeCun, Bottou, Bengio
    and Haffner, 1998).
  - AlexNet: 60 million parameters, 1.2 million training images of ImageNet (Krizhevsky,
    Sutskever and Hinton, 2012).
  - ResNet-50: 1.28 million training images (He, Zhang, Ren and Sun, 2016). The paper does not
    state the number of parameters; the script counts them from the architecture of its
    Table 1 (convolutions without bias, batch normalization, projection shortcuts, the final
    linear layer): 25,557,032, the count of the usual implementations.
  - Inception on CIFAR-10: 1,649,402 parameters (Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht and Vinyals, 2017,
    Table 1), 50,000 training images. This is the network that reaches 100% training accuracy
    on random labels in that table.
  - ViT-H/14: 632 million parameters, pre-trained on the 303 million images of JFT-300M
    (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021, Table 1 and Section 4.1).
  - BERT-large: 340 million parameters; BooksCorpus (800 million words) and English Wikipedia
    (2,500 million words) (Devlin, Chang, Lee and Toutanova, 2019).
  - GPT-3: 175 billion parameters, trained for 300 billion tokens (Brown et al., 2020,
    Section 2.2).
  - Chinchilla: 70 billion parameters, 1.4 trillion training tokens (Hoffmann et al., 2022).
  - The open circle is the digit classifier of lecture 02: 13,002 parameters, 60,000 images.
- `scaling_mnist.pdf`: a miniature scaling experiment. Networks `784 -> h -> h -> 10` (ReLU,
  PyTorch seed 0) trained on the first `N` MNIST training images with the loop of the baseline:
  cross-entropy, Adam with learning rate `1e-3`, batches of 64 reshuffled at every pass
  (seed 1), and 4,690 steps for every run, whatever `N`. The error is measured on the 10,000
  test images after the last step; there is no early stopping and one run per point. Left,
  `h = 16` (13,002 parameters) and `h = 128` (118,282) for
  `N = 500, 1000, 2000, 4000, 8000, 16000, 32000, 60000`. Right, `h = 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256`
  (6,442 to 269,322 parameters) for `N = 60000` and `N = 2000`. The dashed lines have the
  least-squares slopes of the `h = 128` curve (left) and of the `N = 60000` curve (right) on
  logarithmic axes. The point `h = 16`, `N = 60000` is the baseline of the lecture (94.0%
  accuracy). The MNIST files (LeCun, Bottou, Bengio and Haffner, 1998) are downloaded once to
  `notebooks/data/` and are not part of the repository.

### Quotations and published numbers on the slides

- Kaplan et al. (2020), equations (1.1) and (1.2): `L(N) = (N_c/N)^{alpha_N}` with
  `alpha_N ~ 0.076` (non-embedding parameters) and `L(D) = (D_c/D)^{alpha_D}` with
  `alpha_D ~ 0.095` (tokens); models "from 768 to 1.5 billion non-embedding parameters";
  the loss is the cross-entropy in nats per token. The slide writes `P` for their `N` and `N`
  for their `D`.
  "These relations hold across eight orders of magnitude in C_min, six orders of magnitude in
  N, and over two orders of magnitude in D"; "performance depends very weakly on other
  architectural hyperparameters such as depth vs. width". The percentages on the slide
  (`1 - 10^(-0.076) = 16.1%`, `1 - 10^(-0.095) = 19.6%`) are printed by the script.
- Hoffmann et al. (2022): `FLOPs(N, D) ~ 6 N D`; optimal `N ~ C^a`, `D ~ C^b` with
  `a = b = 0.50` (their first approach; 0.49 and 0.51, 0.46 and 0.54 with the other two);
  Chinchilla, 70 billion parameters and 1.4 trillion tokens, against Gopher, 280 billion
  parameters and 300 billion tokens, for the same compute; "the training runs for our analysis
  have all been trained on less than an epoch of data".
- Sutton (2019): the first sentence of the essay, quoted from
  http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html; the remark on symmetries

  paragraph on computer vision ("Modern deep-learning neural networks use only the notions of
