Image sources for the lecture on self-attention and transformers
================================================================

Every figure in this folder is an original plot in the course palette (`notebooks/plotting.py`),
generated by the scripts in `../figures/`, one or more per part of the lecture; `../make_figures.py`
runs them all. The figures have a transparent background, so the colour of the slide shows
through, and are drawn at the size they have on the slide. The diagrams of the slides are TikZ
pictures written inline in the `.tex` file. The scripts print every number that the slides quote.

The language model of the lecture is `../figures/gpt2.py`: GPT-2 small written out in plain
PyTorch (the classes `Block` and `GPT` are the listings of the slides "The block, in code" and
"GPT-2, in code", line by line), its byte-pair tokenizer, and a loader for the weights that
OpenAI published in 2019. Run on its own, the file checks itself: 124,439,808 parameters;
"Hello world" encodes to 15496, 995; after "Attention is all you" the most likely token is
" need" with probability 0.465. The tokenizer agrees with the reference implementation
(`tiktoken`, encoding `gpt2`) on 207,603 tokens of English, German and mixed Unicode text.
The weights (`gpt2-model.safetensors`, 548 MB, from the Hugging Face hub) and the two tokenizer
files (from OpenAI) are downloaded once to `notebooks/data/` and are not part of the repository;
nor is anything in `../data/` (corpora, cached runs, trained weights).

Slides without a script: the opening example (the next token after "Attention is all you":
" need" 46.5%, " have" 17.1%, " can" 6.9%, " get" 4.1%, " want" 2.2%, all others 23.1%) and the
slide "Self-supervised learning" (the probabilities of the tokens of "The dog bites the man.":
0.0001, 0.0009, 0.196, 0.040, 0.069; minus the mean of their logarithms is 4.705) quote GPT-2
through `gpt2.py`; the parameter counts of "Where the parameters are" are printed by `gpt2.py`.
The table of "Attention before 2017" is the summary table of L. Weng's post "Attention?
Attention!" (2018) in the notation of the lecture; the facts of "Since 2017", of "From completing
text to solving problems" and of "Lean as the judge, 2024 to 2026" are from the papers, model
cards and announcements cited on those slides, read in November 2026.

Opening of part 2: recurrent networks against attention
-------------------------------------------------------

Script: `../figures/part0_recurrent.py` (about twenty-five minutes the first time, measured on a busy machine, on two
processes of one thread each; the training results are kept in `../data/recurrent_recall.json` and reused
unless `--train` is given, after which the script takes about two minutes). Three layers of width
`d = 64`, all with PyTorch's default initialization: `nn.RNN(64, 64)` (tanh, 8 320 parameters),
`nn.LSTM(64, 64)` (33 280) and `nn.MultiheadAttention(64, 4)` (16 640).

- `recurrent_panels.pdf`: two panels at the width of the text.
  - Left: the reach of the gradient at initialization. A sequence of `n = 100` random tokens
    `x_t` in `R^64` (N(0, 1) entries) goes through each layer (float64); `y_n` is the output at
    the last position (the state `h_n` of the RNN and of the LSTM, the attention output at
    position `n`, without the residual). Drawn: the operator norm of `d y_n / d x_t` against
    the distance `n - t`, geometric mean over 20 seeds (seed `s` for the weights, `1000 + s`
    for the inputs). Fitted on distances 5 to 60, the RNN loses a factor 0.466 per step
    and the LSTM 0.617; at distance 99 the norms are `2.1e-33` and `4.4e-22`. One attention
    layer stays between `1.3e-2` and `2.0e-2` at every distance `n - t >= 1` (`1/n = 0.01`).
  - Right: "recall the first token". A sequence starts with one of 8 symbols and goes on with
    `n - 1` fillers drawn from 8 other symbols; the model must output the first symbol at the
    last position (chance 12.5%). Embeddings of width 64; the RNN and the LSTM read out their
    last state, the attention model adds a learned position `p_t` to each token and reads out
    `x_n + attention(x)_n` (one layer, 4 heads). One budget for all three: Adam with learning
    rate `3e-3`, gradients clipped to norm 1, batches of 64 fresh sequences, at most 2000
    steps, stopped early once a fresh batch of 512 is all correct. Accuracy on the same 4096
    test sequences, mean of 3 seeds (0, 1, 2), for `n = 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200`.
    Mean accuracy for `n = 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200`: RNN 100, 100, 74.8, 10.9, 12.8, 13.5%;
    LSTM 100, 100, 41.7, 12.5, 12.6, 12.5%; attention 100% for every `n`, each run stopped after
    100 steps. At `n = 20` the RNN learns the task for 2 seeds of 3 (100%, 24.4%, 100%), the
    LSTM for 1 of 3 (12.6%, 100%, 12.5%). Printed only: an LSTM whose forget gates start open
    (bias + 1, as recommended by Jozefowicz et al., 2015; seed 0) learns `n = 20` (100% after
    300 steps) but not `n = 50` (12.0%) or `n = 100` (12.6%).
- Printed only, and quoted on the slide "Recurrence against attention, per layer": the time of one
  forward pass of `nn.RNN(64, 64)` and of `nn.MultiheadAttention(64, 4)` on one sequence of `n`
  tokens, `n = 16, 64, 256, 1024, 4096`, with 1 and 4 threads, the minimum over repetitions
  (about one second of them, at least 5). Two runs on an idle 16-core machine: `nn.RNN` at
  `n = 1024` 7.2 and 7.1 ms with one thread, 7.3 and 7.2 ms with four (7 microseconds per token,
  no faster with more threads); attention at `n = 1024` 8.8 and 8.8 ms with one thread, 2.6 and
  2.6 ms with four; at `n = 4096` attention is the slower one on this CPU (138 and 141 ms against
  31 and 30 ms on one thread): it does `n / d = 64` times more operations. The table of that
  slide is Table 1 of Vaswani et al. (2017), the rows for recurrent and self-attention layers.

Part 1: tokens
--------------

Script: `../figures/part1_tokenizer.py` (two to three minutes).

- Byte-pair encoding (no figure on the slide; the slide "Byte-pair encoding: merge the most
  frequent pair" shows a listing, its output and a table). The algorithm of Gage (1994), used
  for words by Sennrich et al. (2016) and on bytes by GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019), learned on
  Shakespeare (`notebooks/data/tinyshakespeare.txt`, 1,115,394 characters, all ASCII, so as
  many bytes). The text is cut into words with the space before them (the pattern
  ` ?\w+| ?[^\w\s]+|\s+`, a simplification of the one of GPT-2): 294,763 words, 15,057
  distinct (` the` 5,370 times, `the` 72 times). Every word starts as its bytes; the most
  frequent pair of neighbouring symbols, counted over all words with their counts, becomes a
  new symbol; repeat. Merges never cross a word. The listing on the slide runs verbatim in the
  script (it reads the file as Latin-1, so that a byte is a character and a merge is
  `str.replace`) and prints the first 12 merges with the number of times the pair occurs:
  ` t` 23835, `he` 18203, ` a` 13541, `ou` 12730, ` s` 12287, ` m` 10783, `in` 10606,
  ` w` 10546, `re` 9843, `ha` 9673, `nd` 8730, ` the` 8683. A faster version of the same
  algorithm (it updates the counts of the pairs instead of recounting them) agrees with the
  listing on the first 100 merges, pairs and counts, and runs until every word is one symbol:
  21,279 merges (merge 100 is `IN`, 1313 times; merge 1000 `Do`, 89; merge 10,000 ` riot`, 2;
  the last, ` Mediterranean`, 1). Ties between pairs with the same count are broken by the
  order in which the pairs were counted, which differs between the two versions; that matters
  only where many pairs are equally frequent, after the first few hundred merges.

  cut after 0, 100, 1000 and 10,000 merges, and the length of the text in symbols over its

Part 2: attention
-----------------

Script: `../figures/part2_attention.py` (about a minute; no training, no data). Every figure is
drawn at its size on the slide.

- `lookup_weights.pdf`: one query `q` at 50 degrees and eight keys `k_s` at `45 (s - 1)`
  degrees, unit vectors in the plane (left); on the right the weights
  `softmax_s(beta <q, k_s>)` for `beta = 1, 4, 16` and the hard limit (all the weight on
  `k_2`, the key closest to `q`). The largest weight is 0.267, 0.594, 0.972 and 1. The script
  also runs the listing of the slide (a Python dict next to the soft look-up, with the gradient
  in `q`) and prints its output.
- `scaling_softmax.pdf`: `n = 64` queries and keys with independent N(0, 1) entries in `R^d`,
  `d = 4, 8, ..., 1024`, 200 draws for each `d` (12 800 rows; the set-up of section 2 of
  `notebooks/15_transformers.ipynb`, which plots the entropy of the rows; the script prints it
  too). Left: the largest weight of a row of `softmax(<q, k>)` (blue) and of
  `softmax(<q, k>/sqrt d)` (orange), mean over the rows: at `d = 1024`, 0.947 and 0.107 (the
  uniform row has 1/64 = 0.016). Right: the median over the rows of the Frobenius norm of the
  Jacobian of the softmax, `diag(a) - a a^T`: at `d = 1024`, `2.8e-4` without the factor and
  0.18 with it; 55.2% of the rows are below `1e-3` without the factor, none with it. The mean
  (printed) falls less, to 0.070, because it is carried by the few rows whose two largest
  scores nearly tie. The empirical variance of `<q, k>` is 1022.9 at `d = 1024`.
- `equivariance_x.pdf`, `equivariance_Px.pdf`, `equivariance_fx.pdf`, `equivariance_fPx.pdf`: the
  four corners of the commutative square. Six tokens in `R^8` (N(0, 1) entries, seed 0), one per
  row, blue negative and orange positive (one colour scale for `x` and `Px`, one for `f(x)` and
  `f(Px)`), with the index of the token beside each row. `f(x) = x + attention(x)` for
  `nn.MultiheadAttention(8, 2)` with PyTorch's default initialization (seed 0), as in the frame
  "Six layers, and what they assume" of lecture 03; `P` takes row `t` to token
  `pi(t)`, `pi = (1, 4, 2, 6, 3, 5)` (`torch.randperm`, seed 0). Printed:
  `max |f(Px) - P f(x)| = 1.2e-7` (float32) and `max |a(Px) - P a(x) P^T| = 3.0e-8`; with
  the causal mask the difference is 0.244 (the update `f(x) - x` is at most 0.283). With
  sinusoidal positions `p_t` added to the tokens (Vaswani et al., 2017), the updates
  `attention(x + p)` differ by up to 0.137 (they are at most 0.402); the updates are compared,
  since `f(x + p)` contains `x + p` and `p - Pp` alone would break the identity.
- `mask_matrices.pdf`: the six tokens of "The dog bites the man ." as random vectors in `R^16`
  and a random layer, `Q, K, V` with N(0, 1/16) entries: the tensors of the listing of the
  slide (seed 0), not a trained model. From left to right the scores
  `<Q x_t, K x_s> / sqrt(16)` (blue negative, orange positive), the mask (0 on and below the
  diagonal, `-inf` above it) and the weights after the softmax, lower triangular, each row
  summing to 1. The script runs the listing, which checks that changing the last token changes
  no output at an earlier position with the mask (`True`) and does without it (`False`).

Parts 1 to 3: GPT-2 from the inside
-----------------------------------

Scripts: `../figures/part1_embeddings.py` (tokens and positions; about 20 s),
`../figures/part2_gpt2_attention.py` (attention weights; about 20 s) and
`../figures/part3_sampling.py` (temperature and samples; about 3 minutes, most of it the
generated continuations, as `gpt2.generate` runs the whole model again for every token). All

al. (2023), whose list of heads runs from 0.1 to 11.10.

Part 3: tokens as interacting particles
---------------------------------------

Script: `../figures/part3_particles.py` (about twenty-five seconds on one thread). It prints every
number that the frames "Depth as time: tokens are interacting particles" and "Depth as time in
detail: clustering" quote.

- `particles_circle.pdf`: the self-attention dynamics with layer normalization of Geshkovski,
  Letrouit, Polyanskiy and Rigollet (Bull. AMS 62 (2025), equation (SA)), on the circle (`d = 2`),
  with `Q = K = V = I`: one layer is `x_t <- x_t + h sum_s a_ts(x) x_s`, then `x_t <- x_t / |x_t|`,
  with `a_ts(x) = softmax_s(beta <x_t, x_s>)`; to first order in `h` an Euler step of
  `dx_t/dtau = P_perp(x_t) sum_s a_ts(x) x_s`, so layer `k` is the time `tau = k h`.
  `n = 32` tokens, `beta = 16`, `h = 0.1`, 20 000 layers. The initial angles are uniform in
  `(-0.49 pi, 0.49 pi)` (numpy `default_rng(3)`), from -87.9 to 83.5 degrees: all tokens in an
  open half-circle, the hypothesis of Lemma 6.4 of that paper (one cluster in the limit,
  exponentially fast, for every `beta > 0`). Drawn at 108 x 38 mm.
  - Left: the tokens on the circle at layers 0, 100, 1000 and 20 000, with the number of
    tokens in each cluster (13, 12, 7; then 13, 19; then 32).
  - Right: the angle of every token against the layer, on a log scale from 1 to 20 000; dotted
    lines at layers 100, 1000 and 20 000. Each token is coloured by its cluster at layer 200.
  - Clusters (two tokens in one cluster if their angles differ by less than 1e-3 rad): 32 at
    layer 0, 28 at layer 50, 3 from layer 99, 2 from layer 290, 1 from layer 14 017. The angular
    diameter of the configuration is 2.99 rad at layer 0, 1.80 at 100, 1.21 at 1000 and exactly 0
    (in floating point) at 20 000.
  - The interaction energy `E_beta = (1/(2 beta n^2)) sum_{t,s} exp(beta <x_t, x_s>)`, divided by
    its maximum `e^beta/(2 beta)`, is 0.2000 at layer 0, 0.3548 at 100, 0.5176 at 1000 and 1 at
    20 000. It increases at every layer of the discrete scheme, up to rounding (smallest change
    -6.7e-15), as equation (3.6) of the paper says it does along the flow.
  - Choice of the parameters: after a scan (beta from 4 to 36, initial arcs from 0.9 pi to 2 pi,
    seeds 0-5) so that both time scales fit in the layers drawn. With these parameters the

Appendix: vision transformers
-----------------------------

Script: `../figures/appendix_vit.py` (about 30 minutes on three CPU threads of a shared machine the first time; the
runs are cached in `../data/vit/runs.json` and the trained model in `../data/vit/vit_60000.pt`,
both git-ignored, after which the script draws the figures in seconds). It prints every number
that the slides of the appendix quote. The MNIST files (LeCun, Bottou, Bengio and Haffner, 1998)
are downloaded once to `notebooks/data/` and are not part of the repository.

The model is the vision transformer of Dosovitskiy et al. (2021, arXiv:2010.11929, Eq. 1-4),
small: the 28 x 28 digit is cut into 16 patches of 7 x 7 pixels, read row by row
(`img.view(-1, 28, 28).unfold(1, 7, 7).unfold(2, 7, 7)`); each patch `pi_t in R^49` is mapped to
`E pi_t in R^64` (a linear layer with bias), a learned position vector `p_t` is added
(`p_0, ..., p_16`, initialized `N(0, 0.02^2)`, seed 0), and a learned class token (initialized
to 0) is put in front at `t = 0`. Then 4 blocks of part 3 (the class `Block` of `gpt2.py`,
copied, without the two lines of the causal mask: `x <- x + Attention(LN(x))`,
`x <- x + MLP(LN(x))`, 4 heads, an MLP of width 256 with the tanh GELU), a layer norm and a
linear read-out of the class token to 10 scores. 205,066 parameters. The class token, not
the mean of the patch tokens: it is the choice of the paper (its Appendix D.3 finds both work
equally well with different learning rates), and it gives the model one token whose attention
over the patches can be drawn. Patches of 7 x 7 (16 tokens) rather than 4 x 4 (49 tokens): in a
pilot epoch on three threads, 48 s and 5.6% test error against 124 s and 9.6%.

Training, for every network: the loop of lecture 02 (cross-entropy, Adam with learning rate
`1e-3`, batches of 64 reshuffled at every pass with seed 1, weights from seed 0) for 4,690
steps, the 5 epochs of the baseline, on the first `N` training digits, whatever `N`; as
`part2_mnist.py` and `part5_scale.py` of lecture 04. One run per point; the test error is
measured on the 10,000 test digits after the last step, with no early stopping. The two
reference networks of lecture 04, `784 -> 128 -> 128 -> 10` (118,282 parameters) and the
convolutional network with 8 and 16 channels (5,994 parameters), are trained again by the
same loop on one thread, as in lecture 04; the vision transformer on three threads.

- `vit_digit.pdf`: test digit 1 of MNIST (a 2), 34 mm wide, blue ink on white as in lecture 04,
  cut into its 16 patches, numbered in reading order.
- `vit_sequence.pdf`: the same 16 patches as a sequence, `t = 1, ..., 16`, after the class
  token at `t = 0` (an orange box: a learned vector, no pixels).
- `vit_data.pdf`: test error against the number `N` of training digits (1,000, 4,000, 16,000,
  60,000; logarithmic axes) for the three networks.
- `vit_positions.pdf` (not on the slides): the cosine similarity of the learned position
  vectors of the vision transformer trained on 60,000 digits, as in Figure 7 (centre) of the
  paper: tile `(r, c)` shows `cos(p_t, p_s)` for the patch `t` in row `r` and column `c`
  (framed) and every patch `s`, laid out on the 4 x 4 grid; blue negative, orange positive,
  from -1 to 1. Unlike in the paper, it shows no grid: the mean similarity with the 4
  neighbours is -0.09, with the patches at distance 3 or more 0.03.
- `vit_attention.pdf`: the attention `a_0s` of the class token over the 16 patches, averaged
  over the 4 heads, in the first and the last block, for test digits 1, 2, 3, 4, 7 (a 2, a 1,
  a 0, a 4, a 9), drawn in grey; the orange of a patch is proportional to its weight, relative
  to the largest.
